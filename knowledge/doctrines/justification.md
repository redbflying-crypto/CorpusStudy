# Definition

Justification is God's act of declaring a sinner righteous in his sight. Paul states the conclusion plainly: "a man is justified by faith without the deeds of the law."[^kjv-rom3-28] God "justifieth the ungodly", and the faith of the one who "worketh not, but believeth" is "counted for righteousness".[^kjv-rom4-5] Its fruit is peace: "being justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ".[^kjv-rom5-1]

# Reformed view

In Reformed teaching justification is forensic: a verdict of God's court, not a change worked inside the believer. Calvin reads Genesis 15:6 this way. Abraham "acquired (adeptus sit) righteousness before God, and that by imputation", and God "approves as righteous those to whom he imputes righteousness".[^calvin-gen15]

The ground of the verdict is Christ's work, not the believer's. God "hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him."[^kjv-2cor5-21] Matthew Henry explains that this righteousness is "put upon them as a crown, as a robe". It is free grace to the sinner, but "Christ bought it, and paid the price".[^henry-rom3]

Faith itself is not the righteousness that justifies. Henry calls it "the appointed means of uniting them to Him" who is "the Lord our Righteousness".[^henry-rom4] Even faith is God's gift: "by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God".[^kjv-eph2-8]

Justification and [sanctification](/doctrines/sanctification.md) are distinct but never separated. The same passage says believers are "created in Christ Jesus unto good works".[^kjv-eph2-8] In the same way James's "by works a man is justified, and not by faith only"[^kjv-jas2-24] is read as showing what true faith is. Henry writes that "a justifying faith cannot be without works", while "true faith alone ... saves their souls".[^henry-jas2] Henry adds that the one reconciled to God is "created anew": "his heart is not merely set right, but a new heart is given him".[^henry-2cor5]

# Where other traditions differ

These are summaries for orientation, not drawn from the corpus:

- **Lutheran** teaching shares justification by faith alone and imputation, though it frames the relation of law and gospel differently.
- **Roman Catholic** teaching, as defined at the Council of Trent, understands justification to include the inner renewal of the person by infused grace. On that view it can grow through good works and can be lost by mortal sin.
- **Eastern Orthodox** theology speaks less in legal terms and more of union with God (theosis).
- **Arminian and Wesleyan** traditions affirm justification by faith. They differ from Calvinists on how faith arises and on whether a justified person can finally fall away (see [perseverance](/doctrines/perseverance.md)).

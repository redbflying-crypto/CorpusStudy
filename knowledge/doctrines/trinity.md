# Definition

The doctrine of the Trinity confesses that there is one God, who exists eternally as three persons: the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. Scripture insists on God's unity: "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD".[^kjv-deut6-4] Yet it also names three. Christ sends his disciples to baptize "in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost",[^kjv-matt28-19] and Paul blesses the church with "The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, and the love of God, and the communion of the Holy Ghost".[^kjv-2cor13-14]

The Son is God: "the Word was with God, and the Word was God", and "All things were made by him".[^kjv-john1-1] The Spirit is God: Peter tells Ananias, who had lied "to the Holy Ghost", that he has "not lied unto men, but unto God."[^kjv-acts5-3] At Jesus' baptism all three are present: the Son comes up out of the water, the Spirit descends "like a dove", and the Father says, "This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased."[^kjv-matt3-16]

# Reformed view

Reformed churches receive the doctrine of the ancient creeds and ground it in Scripture. Matthew Henry describes the Christian as giving himself to God "as the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost, three Persons but one God".[^henry-matt28]

The three persons act together in salvation. Henry reads Paul's blessing as covering what "Christ of his free grace and favour has purchased; the Father out of his free love has purposed; and the Holy Ghost applies and bestows."[^henry-2cor13] See [grace](/themes/grace.md).

Henry says of John 1: "What the evangelist says of Christ proves that he is God."[^henry-john1] Calvin finds in John 17:5 that Christ "is not only God eternal but also the eternal Word of God begotten of the Father before all ages."[^calvin-john17]

Reformed interpreters do not always agree on which texts prove the doctrine. Henry thinks the plural word for God in Deuteronomy 6:4 seems "plainly to intimate a Trinity of persons, even in this express declaration of the unity of the Godhead."[^henry-deut6] Calvin, on the same plural in Genesis 1:1, says it "does not seem a solid proof for so great a truth", although Scripture "always calls us back to the Father, his Word, and the Spirit."[^calvin-gen1] On the threefold "Holy, holy, holy" of Isaiah 6 he does "not doubt that the one God in three Persons is here meant", yet "clearer passages should be used in defending our faith".[^calvin-isa6]

# Where other traditions differ

These are summaries for orientation, not drawn from the corpus:

- **Shared ground.** Protestants, Roman Catholics and Eastern Orthodox all confess the Nicene faith (Nicaea 325, Constantinople 381): the Son is of one essence with the Father, and the Spirit is worshipped and glorified with the Father and the Son.
- **Eastern Orthodox** churches reject the *filioque* (Latin for *and the Son*), the Western addition to the creed saying that the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son. Catholics, Lutherans and the Reformed keep it; the Orthodox hold that the Spirit proceeds from the Father as the one source, and object to its addition without a common council.
- **Non-trinitarian groups** reject the doctrine as the historic churches define it: Jehovah's Witnesses (the Son created, the Spirit an impersonal force), Oneness Pentecostals (one person revealed as Father, Son and Spirit), Latter-day Saints (three distinct divine beings) and Unitarians (God one person only).

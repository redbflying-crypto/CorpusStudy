# Definition

The atonement is the work of Christ in his death by which sinners are reconciled to God. Isaiah foretold a servant "wounded for our transgressions" and "bruised for our iniquities", on whom "the LORD hath laid ... the iniquity of us all".[^kjv-isa53-5] God set Christ forth "to be a propitiation through faith in his blood", "that he might be just, and the justifier of him which believeth in Jesus."[^kjv-rom3-25] By his own blood Christ entered the holy place, "having obtained eternal redemption for us."[^kjv-heb9-12]

# Reformed view

Reformed teaching understands the cross first as penal substitution: Christ bore in the sinner's place the penalty that sin deserves. He "his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree".[^kjv-1pet2-24] Henry explains that "he bore the punishment of them, and thereby satisfied Divine justice."[^henry-1pet2] Calvin reads Isaiah 53:5 the same way: "Christ paid the price of our chastisement, that is, the chastisement due to us", and "the penalty for our sins was transferred to Christ."[^calvin-isa53-5] He adds that this "refers of course only to the punishment of sin, for he was free from all guilt."[^calvin-isa53-6] God "made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin".[^kjv-2cor5-21]

The atonement satisfies God's justice. God "hates sin, when nothing less than the blood of Christ would satisfy for it".[^henry-rom3] Calvin: "He alone satisfies the Father; he alone reconciles us to him."[^calvin-isa53-5] The sacrifice is complete and unrepeatable. Christ "offered one sacrifice for sins for ever", and "by one offering he hath perfected for ever them that are sanctified."[^kjv-heb10-12] Calvin contrasts the high priest's yearly expiation, which "was ineffectual and had to be repeated each year", with Christ's, which "kept its vigor forever".[^calvin-heb9]

Substitution is central but not the whole. Henry also names "Sin and Satan, death and hell, the world and the flesh" as "the strong foes he has vanquished".[^henry-isa53] Calvin says "Christ conquered death, the world, and the devil."[^calvin-isa53-12]

On the extent of the atonement, the Canons of Dort (II.8) and the Westminster Confession (VIII.8) teach definite or particular atonement: Christ died to secure the salvation of the elect, and his death certainly saves those for whom it was intended. Its defenders point to texts such as "the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep"[^kjv-john10-11] and "he shall save his people from their sins."[^kjv-matt1-21] Henry writes that "There are many whom Christ justifies, even as many as he gave his life a ransom for", while also saying that Christ "took away the sin of the world" and that one sacrifice "is enough to procure for all nations and ages, spiritual pardon".[^henry-isa53][^henry-heb10] Reformed writers have usually expressed this as a death sufficient for all and effective for the elect. This is the most debated point of the doctrine within the Reformed family (see below).

# Where other traditions differ

These are summaries for orientation, not drawn from the corpus:

- **Within the Reformed family**, hypothetical universalists (such as John Davenant, a British delegate at Dort) and **Amyraldians** (the school of Saumur) hold that Christ died for all people in one sense, conditionally on faith, and for the elect effectually. Historians also debate how far Calvin himself held the later doctrine of definite atonement.
- **Arminian** and **Lutheran** traditions teach universal atonement: Christ died for every person (1 John 2:2; 1 Tim 2:6), and his benefits are received by faith. Many in both traditions also affirm substitution and satisfaction.
- The **governmental** view (Hugo Grotius; later New England theology) holds that Christ's death upholds God's moral government and shows the seriousness of sin, so that God may justly forgive, rather than paying the exact penalty owed.
- **Moral influence** views (associated with Peter Abelard and much liberal Protestantism) see the cross chiefly as a revelation of God's love that moves sinners to repentance.
- **Christus Victor** emphases (named by Gustaf Aulén, drawing on early church writers) present the cross chiefly as Christ's victory over sin, death and the devil. Eastern Orthodox theology often frames the atonement this way.

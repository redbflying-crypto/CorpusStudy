# Definition

Sanctification is the work of God by which those he justifies are set apart to him and made increasingly holy. It has a once-for-all side: "ye are washed, but ye are sanctified, but ye are justified in the name of the Lord Jesus, and by the Spirit of our God."[^kjv-1cor6-11] It also has a growing side: believers "are changed into the same image from glory to glory, even as by the Spirit of the Lord."[^kjv-2cor3-18] It is God's declared purpose for his people: "this is the will of God, even your sanctification".[^kjv-1thess4-3]

# Reformed view

Reformed teaching keeps sanctification distinct from [justification](/doctrines/justification.md) but never separate from it. Henry puts it plainly: "Our justification is owing to the suffering and merit of Christ; our sanctification to the working of the Holy Spirit; but both go together. All who are made righteous in the sight of God, are made holy by the grace of God."[^henry-1cor6]

Its root is union with Christ in his death and resurrection. "Our old man is crucified with him, that the body of sin might be destroyed, that henceforth we should not serve sin."[^kjv-rom6-6] Henry says the old nature is crucified "in every true believer, by the grace derived from the cross", and is now "weakened and in a dying state, though it yet struggles for life".[^henry-rom6] Calvin draws the consequence from Romans 12:1: "the Lord has redeemed us for no other purpose than that we may consecrate ourselves and all our members to him", so that "our whole life should be an exercise in holiness".[^calvin-rom12]

Sanctification is God's work in which the believer is active, not passive. "Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling. For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure."[^kjv-phil2-12] Henry: "The working of God's grace in us, is to quicken and engage our endeavours."[^henry-phil2] God works through appointed means, above all his word: "Sanctify them through thy truth: thy word is truth."[^kjv-john17-17] Henry adds that "Even disciples must pray for sanctifying grace", and that "The real holiness of all true Christians is the fruit of Christ's death".[^henry-john17]

The work is never complete in this life. Henry writes that "the best are sanctified but in part while in this world; therefore we should pray for, and press toward, complete holiness."[^henry-1thess5] Calvin rejects the claim "that we can be perfect in this life": "Perfection is the goal toward which we strive throughout the course of our lives, and do not attain until the race is done."[^calvin-eph1] "If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves".[^kjv-1john1-8] Henry, reading Romans 7 as the struggle of a regenerate man, still finds hope in it: "Sin dwelling in a man, does not prove its ruling, or having dominion over him."[^henry-rom7]

# Where other traditions differ

These are summaries for orientation, not drawn from the corpus:

- **Wesleyan and Holiness** traditions teach entire sanctification, or Christian perfection: a work of grace, usually after conversion, by which a believer can be made perfect in love in this life. It frees the believer from wilful sin, though not from ignorance, mistakes or weakness.
- **Roman Catholic** teaching does not separate justification from sanctification. Justification itself includes inner renewal by infused, sanctifying grace, first given in baptism and increased through the sacraments and good works. Mortal sin destroys it and penance restores it, and purgatory completes the purification of those who die in grace.
- The **Keswick** or **Higher Life** movement teaches that after conversion believers may enter, by a crisis of full surrender and faith, a victorious life in which the Spirit restrains the sinful nature; it stresses resting in Christ more than effort.

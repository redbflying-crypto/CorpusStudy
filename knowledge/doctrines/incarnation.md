# Definition

The incarnation is the act by which the eternal Son of God took a true human nature and became man, without ceasing to be God. John writes that "the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us".[^kjv-john1-14] Paul says that Christ, "being in the form of God", "took upon him the form of a servant, and was made in the likeness of men", and "humbled himself, and became obedient unto death".[^kjv-phil2-6] He was conceived by the Holy Ghost, so that "that holy thing which shall be born of thee shall be called the Son of God."[^kjv-luke1-35] Paul calls it the "mystery of godliness: God was manifest in the flesh".[^kjv-1tim3-16]

Hebrews gives the purpose. He "took part of the same" flesh and blood "that through death he might destroy him that had the power of death", and it "behoved him to be made like unto his brethren, that he might be a merciful and faithful high priest".[^kjv-heb2-14]

# Reformed view

With the whole church since Chalcedon, Reformed theology confesses one person in two natures. Matthew Henry tells the reader to "Notice the two natures of Christ; his Divine nature, and human nature." In his human nature "he became like us in all things except sin", and his exaltation "was of Christ's human nature, in union with the Divine."[^henry-phil2] Henry writes that "it was so ordered that Christ should partake of our nature, yet that he should be pure from the defilement of original sin", and adds a caution: "The mystery of Christ's becoming man is to be adored, not curiously inquired into."[^henry-matt1]

Calvin stresses how real and how low the humanity was. By the word flesh John "expresses himself more forcibly than if he had said He was made man": the Son "stooped so low as to take upon himself this same flesh which is subject to so many miseries".[^calvin-john1-14] Christ "put on not only our flesh but also our human feeling", and "his fear was real and not fictitious."[^calvin-john12-27]

The incarnation serves [the atonement](/doctrines/atonement.md). Henry notes that "the nature of angels could not be an atoning sacrifice for the sin of man", and that Christ "became man, and was tempted, that he might be every way qualified to succour his people".[^henry-heb2] His life on earth is told in [the life of Jesus](/events/life-of-jesus.md); see also [Jesus](/people/jesus.md).

# Where other traditions differ

These are summaries for orientation, not drawn from the corpus:

- **Shared ground.** Roman Catholics, Eastern Orthodox, Lutherans and the Reformed all confess the Council of Chalcedon (451): one Christ in two natures, without confusion or change, without division or separation. The Oriental Orthodox churches (Coptic, Armenian, Ethiopian, Syriac) did not accept Chalcedon, though modern dialogues have found much agreement.
- **Lutheran and Reformed** churches both hold Chalcedon but differ on the communication of attributes. Lutheran teaching holds that divine properties are communicated to Christ's human nature, so that his humanity can be present wherever he wills, which supports their teaching on his bodily presence in the Supper. Reformed teaching stresses that each nature keeps its own properties: what belongs to either nature is said of the one person, but his human body remains finite and is now in heaven, while the Son is not confined to his flesh (the so-called *extra calvinisticum*). See [sacraments](/doctrines/sacraments.md).
- **Kenotic** theories (since the nineteenth century) hold that the Son laid aside some divine attributes in becoming man; Reformed theology reads Philippians 2 as taking a servant's form, not giving up deity.

# Definition

The perseverance of the saints is the teaching that God keeps those he has truly brought to faith, so that they continue in grace and are finally saved. Christ says of his sheep: "they shall never perish, neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand".[^kjv-john10-27] Paul is "confident of this very thing, that he which hath begun a good work in you will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ".[^kjv-phil1-6] Believers "are kept by the power of God through faith unto salvation".[^kjv-1pet1-5]

# Reformed view

In Reformed teaching perseverance rests on God's keeping, not on the believer's strength. Calvin draws from John 10:28 that "the salvation of all the elect is as certain as that God's power is invincible", for "Our own faith is unfirm and we ourselves tend greatly to waver."[^calvin-john10] He adds elsewhere that "the stability of our salvation is not in us but in the secret election of God."[^calvin-matt24] Nothing "shall be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord."[^kjv-rom8-38] Henry: "None can take Christ from the believer: none can take the believer from Him".[^henry-rom8] Of those the Father gave to the Son, Henry writes, "he has lost none, nor will lose any one."[^henry-jude] The doctrine follows from [election](/doctrines/election.md). On Romans 8:30 Henry writes that once corruption is broken in effectual calling and guilt removed in justification, "nothing can come between that soul and glory."[^henry-rom8-28]

Perseverance belongs to those truly regenerate, not to every outward professor. Henry says God will complete his work "in every soul wherein he has really begun it by regeneration; though we must not trust in outward appearances".[^henry-phil1] John explains those who leave: "They went out from us, but they were not of us; for if they had been of us, they would no doubt have continued with us".[^kjv-1john2-19]

The warnings of Scripture are real. Hebrews says it is "impossible" to renew to repentance those who "were once enlightened" and "shall fall away".[^kjv-heb6-4] Reformed interpreters read such passages in two complementary ways. Henry holds that the passage does not "prove that any one who is made a new creature in Christ, ever becomes a final apostate from him", and he notes the writer's own confidence of "better things" in his readers.[^henry-heb6][^kjv-heb6-9] Warnings are also means by which God keeps his people. Calvin says Christ's warning about deceivers "is added to fill the faithful with fear, and so to make them more watchful and on their guard".[^calvin-matt24] When Christ exhorts us to perseverance, "we must not rely on our own energy and industry; we must rather pray him who commands us to confirm us in his love."[^calvin-john15] So Henry urges diligence "in the use of all the means which lead to our salvation, persevering therein to the end."[^henry-phil2]

# Where other traditions differ

These are summaries for orientation, not drawn from the corpus:

- **Arminian and Wesleyan** traditions hold that a true believer can, by persistent unbelief or sin, finally fall away and be lost. Grace preserves, but it can be resisted; some Arminians limit final apostasy to deliberate repudiation of faith.
- **Lutheran** teaching holds that saving faith can be lost, for example through unrepentant sin or unbelief, and regained by repentance, while stressing that God's promises in word and sacrament sustain faith. Lutherans also affirm that the elect will be saved.
- **Roman Catholic** teaching holds that the grace of justification is lost by mortal sin and can be restored through the sacrament of penance. Final perseverance is a gift of God that no one can know with certainty without special revelation (Council of Trent).
- Some **evangelical** writers, notably in the *Free Grace* movement, teach *once saved, always saved* without the Reformed stress on continuing faith and holiness; Reformed writers distinguish perseverance from that view.

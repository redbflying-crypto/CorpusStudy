# Definition

A sacrament is a holy sign and seal, appointed by God, by which he confirms the promises of his covenant to faith. The pattern is circumcision: Abraham "received the sign of circumcision, a seal of the righteousness of the faith which he had yet being uncircumcised".[^kjv-rom4-11] Christ appointed two such signs for the church: baptism "in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost"[^kjv-matt28-19], and the Supper: "this is my body, which is broken for you: this do in remembrance of me."[^kjv-1cor11-23]

# Reformed view

In Reformed teaching a sacrament is always joined to the word. Calvin asks, "what is a sacrament unless the Word come before it? Therefore, the symbol is an accessory to the Word."[^calvin-heb9] It is received by faith: "The sacraments become effective through faith; and men's unworthiness does not lessen their effect." Yet the signs are not empty: "Baptism is the water of rebirth, though the whole world disbelieve."[^calvin-ezek20]

Baptism signifies and seals union with Christ, cleansing and new life. Henry writes: "Baptism is an outward sign of that inward washing, or sanctification of the Spirit, which seals and evidences the believer's justification."[^henry-matt28] Paul links it to circumcision: believers are "circumcised with the circumcision made without hands", "Buried with him in baptism".[^kjv-col2-11]

Reformed churches baptize the infants of believers as well as adult converts. They argue from the continuity of the covenant of grace, which God made "between me and thee and thy seed after thee".[^kjv-gen17-7] Peter told his hearers that "the promise is unto you, and to your children".[^kjv-acts2-39] Henry says circumcision sealed "of heaven, through Christ, to the whole church of God", and that "still the encouraging promises are to us and our children".[^henry-gen17][^henry-acts2] Households were baptized together: the jailer "was baptized, he and all his, straightway."[^kjv-acts16-33] The sign does not guarantee the reality: "The outward sign is for the visible church; the inward seal of the Spirit is peculiar to those whom God knows to be believers".[^henry-gen17]

"The cup of blessing which we bless, is it not the communion of the blood of Christ?"[^kjv-1cor10-16] The Reformed view, associated with Calvin, is often called a spiritual real presence: by the power of the Spirit, believers truly receive Christ through faith. Calvin holds both that "The Table of Christ is the communion of his body and blood" and that "sacraments are never without the power of the Spirit except when men make themselves unfit to receive the grace offered."[^calvin-ezek20] It does not depend on a change in the elements: Henry notes that Paul "did not mean that the bread was changed into flesh", yet the Supper is "not merely in remembrance of Christ".[^henry-1cor11] "We partake of the sun, not by having the sun put into our hands, but the beams of it darted down upon us; so we partake of Christ by partaking of his grace".[^henry-matt26]

# Where other traditions differ

These are summaries for orientation, not drawn from the corpus:

- **Roman Catholic** teaching counts seven sacraments, which confer grace *ex opere operato* (by the rite validly performed) where the recipient puts no obstacle in the way. Baptism regenerates and removes original sin. In the Eucharist the substance of bread and wine becomes Christ's body and blood (transubstantiation), and the Mass makes present the one sacrifice of Calvary.
- **Lutheran** teaching holds that Christ's true body and blood are present *in, with and under* the bread and wine (the sacramental union) and are received by all who eat, believers or not. Baptism, including infant baptism, is a means of regeneration through the word joined to the water.
- The **Zwinglian** or memorial view (Ulrich Zwingli, and many evangelicals since) sees the Supper chiefly as a remembrance of Christ's death and a pledge of the church's faith, with Christ present to faith in his divinity but not bodily. Zwingli himself defended infant baptism.
- **Baptist** churches baptize only those who profess faith (credobaptism), usually by immersion, noting that the New Testament gives no explicit command or example of infant baptism. **Reformed Baptists** (Second London Confession, 1689) share Reformed teaching on grace and often Calvin's view of the Supper, but hold that the sign of the new covenant belongs to professing believers.

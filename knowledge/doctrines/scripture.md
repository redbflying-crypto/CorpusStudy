# Definition

The authority of Scripture is the teaching that the Bible, as God's inspired word, is the supreme and sufficient rule of faith and life. "All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness: That the man of God may be perfect, throughly furnished unto all good works." The same Scriptures "are able to make thee wise unto salvation through faith which is in Christ Jesus."[^kjv-2tim3-15] Their writers did not speak on their own initiative: "the prophecy came not in old time by the will of man: but holy men of God spake as they were moved by the Holy Ghost."[^kjv-2pet1-20]

# Reformed view

Scripture's authority rests on its origin in God. (Reformed confessions add that it does not depend on the approval of the church, a point Calvin argues in the Institutes, which are outside this corpus.) Because it is inspired, Calvin says, "men should receive it reverently and without further argument", and "we treat Scripture with the same reverence that we do God, because it is from God alone".[^calvin-2tim3] Henry holds that "the Scriptures are to be accounted the words of the Holy Ghost". Yet the Spirit "so assisted and directed them in delivering what they had received from him, that they clearly expressed what they made known."[^henry-2pet1] The human writers were not passive. Calvin says they were moved "not because they were bereft of their own minds", but "because they did not dare to say anything of their own."[^calvin-2pet1]

How does a believer know that Scripture is God's word? Calvin answers with the inner witness of the Spirit: "The same Spirit who made Moses and the prophets certain of their calling, has now testified to our own hearts that he used them as his servants for our instruction." Without that illumination the majesty of Scripture goes unseen, for "only those illumined by the Spirit have the eyes to see what should be evident to all men".[^calvin-2tim3] Henry writes similarly: "The Christian knows that book to be the word of God, in which he tastes a sweetness, and feels a power, and sees a glory, truly divine."[^henry-2pet1]

Scripture is also sufficient. Calvin reads 2 Timothy 3:17 to mean that "Scripture is adequate and sufficient for our perfecting", so that "anyone who is not satisfied with Scripture, hopes to know more than he needs".[^calvin-2tim3]

From this follows *sola scriptura*: Scripture alone is the final judge of church teaching and tradition. Jesus rebuked those "Making the word of God of none effect through your tradition".[^kjv-mark7-13] Calvin tells believers to judge between teachers "with the Word of God as our only norm", though he also values "Public testing of doctrine" by "the common consent and polity of the church."[^calvin-1john4] He rejects the use of 2 Peter 1:20 "to give their councils alone the right to interpret Scripture", for "the Spirit who has spoken by the prophets is his own interpreter."[^calvin-2pet1]

Scripture is also clear enough for ordinary believers to read with profit. "The entrance of thy words giveth light; it giveth understanding unto the simple."[^kjv-ps119-130] Henry insists that the common people should read the Scriptures, and that "The word of God should not be kept in an unknown tongue".[^henry-1thess5]

# Where other traditions differ

These are summaries for orientation, not drawn from the corpus:

- **Roman Catholic** teaching holds that Scripture and Sacred Tradition together form one deposit of the word of God, which the Magisterium (the pope and the bishops in communion with him) authentically interprets. It affirms the inspiration of Scripture, and its canon includes the deuterocanonical books that Protestants call the Apocrypha.
- **Eastern Orthodox** theology regards Scripture as the highest expression within Holy Tradition, read in the mind of the Church: the Fathers, the ecumenical councils and the liturgy. Authority rests in the Church's conciliar consensus rather than in one teaching office.
- **Liberal and historical-critical** approaches generally treat Scripture as a human witness to revelation or religious experience, open to error and containing diverse theologies, and locate authority in Christ, experience or reason. Neo-orthodox theologians such as Karl Barth speak of Scripture becoming God's word as it witnesses to Christ.

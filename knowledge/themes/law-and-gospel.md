# Overview

God speaks in Scripture both in law, which tells us what he requires, and in gospel, which announces what he has freely given in Christ. "The law was given by Moses, but grace and truth came by Jesus Christ."[^kjv-john1-17] The law is good: "holy, and the commandment holy, and just, and good."[^kjv-rom7-12] Yet "by the deeds of the law there shall no flesh be justified in his sight: for by the law is the knowledge of sin."[^kjv-rom3-20]

# Development through Scripture

The psalmist praises the law as "perfect, converting the soul".[^kjv-ps19-7] Paul calls the law "our schoolmaster to bring us unto Christ, that we might be justified by faith."[^kjv-gal3-24]

The gospel does what the law could not: "what the law could not do, in that it was weak through the flesh", God did by sending his Son, so that "the righteousness of the law might be fulfilled in us".[^kjv-rom8-3] "Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to every one that believeth."[^kjv-rom10-4] Faith does not cancel the law: "Do we then make void the law through faith? God forbid: yea, we establish the law."[^kjv-rom3-31]

# Reformed view

Law and gospel must be distinguished in the matter of [justification](/doctrines/justification.md). Calvin writes that "in the law God demands his due, but does not confer the power to pay it properly", and that "it is the function of the law to uncover the disease; it gives us no hope of its cure. It is the function of the gospel to bring healing to those who are without hope."[^calvin-2cor3-6] The fault lies in us, not in the law: "The law itself does not produce sin; it finds sin in us."[^calvin-acts7]

Yet in Reformed teaching law and gospel are not enemies. Henry says the law was given "to lead men to see their need of the promise", and that "it is not possible that the holy, just, and good law of God ... should be contrary to the gospel of Christ."[^henry-gal3-19] Calvin reconciles David's praise of the law with Paul's: "When Christ gives life to the law, David's praises apply to it; when Christ is taken away, the law is altogether as Paul describes it."[^calvin-2cor3-14]

Reformed theology commonly distinguishes three uses of the law: to restrain evil in society, to convict sinners and drive them to Christ, and to guide believers (a scheme set out in Calvin's Institutes, which is not in this corpus). Believers are freed from the law "as a covenant of works", but Henry adds, "Understand this not of the law as a rule".[^henry-rom7] For the converted, "the law, by the teaching of the Holy Spirit, becomes his loved rule of duty".[^henry-gal3-23] Calvin writes that "a sincere love of the law of God is a sure sign of our adoption because it is a work of the Spirit".[^calvin-ps119]

**Where other traditions differ.** These are summaries for orientation, not drawn from the corpus. **Lutheran** theology makes the law-gospel distinction central to all preaching and stresses that the law always accuses; Lutherans affirm a third use in their confessions but have debated its force. **Antinomian** views deny that the moral law binds believers as a rule of life. **Dispensational** and **New Covenant Theology** writers hold that the Mosaic law as a whole has ended, with believers under the law of Christ. **Roman Catholic** teaching does not separate law and gospel this way; it speaks of a new law of grace written by the Spirit.

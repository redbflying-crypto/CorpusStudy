# Summary

"A certain man went down from Jerusalem to Jericho, and fell among thieves", who left him "half dead". A priest saw him and "passed by on the other side", and a Levite did the same. "But a certain Samaritan, as he journeyed, came where he was: and when he saw him, he had compassion on him". He "bound up his wounds, pouring in oil and wine", took him to an inn, paid the host two pence and promised to repay anything more.[^kjv-luke10-30] Jesus then asked which of the three "was neighbour unto him that fell among the thieves". The lawyer answered, "He that shewed mercy on him", and Jesus said, "Go, and do thou likewise."[^kjv-luke10-36]

# Setting

A lawyer "stood up, and tempted him, saying, Master, what shall I do to inherit eternal life?"[^kjv-luke10-25] Pointed to the law, he answered with its two great commands, joining Deuteronomy, "thou shalt love the LORD thy God with all thine heart",[^kjv-deut6-5] with Leviticus, "thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself".[^kjv-lev19-18] Jesus replied, "Thou hast answered right: this do, and thou shalt live." But the lawyer, "willing to justify himself", asked, "And who is my neighbour?"[^kjv-luke10-25]

The hero of the story is pointed: in Jesus' day "the Jews have no dealings with the Samaritans."[^kjv-john4-9] Henry calls the Samaritan's people "the nation which the Jews most despised and detested".[^henry-luke10]

# Meaning

The lawyer asked whom he must love. Jesus turned the question round and asked who proved to be a neighbour.[^kjv-luke10-36] Matthew Henry calls the parable "a beautiful explanation of the law of loving our neighbour as ourselves, without regard to nation, party, or any other distinction." He also sees it as a rebuke: "how many excuses men will make to avoid trouble or expense in relieving others." The duty is plain: "to succour, help, and relieve all that are in distress and necessity."[^henry-luke10]

Calvin draws the same lesson from the law itself. "Neighbor includes the whole of mankind, as Christ showed in the person of the Samaritan who took pity on an unknown man".[^calvin-lev19] Elsewhere he writes that "when God spoke of our neighbor, he meant to include the whole of the human species."[^calvin-matt5]

# Interpretive notes

In Reformed reading Jesus' "this do, and thou shalt live"[^kjv-luke10-25] holds up the law's demand, and the parable shows how far short the self-justifying lawyer falls. Henry writes that "No one will ever love God and his neighbour with any measure of pure, spiritual love, who is not made a partaker of converting grace." The parable is not a way to earn eternal life (see [law and gospel](/themes/law-and-gospel.md)). Henry also draws a secondary picture of Christ from it: "Satan, our enemy, has robbed us, and wounded us", and "The blessed Jesus had compassion on us." Those who have received that mercy are bidden to "go and do likewise".[^henry-luke10] See [love](/themes/love.md) and [Jesus](/people/jesus.md).

These are summaries for orientation, not drawn from the corpus:

- Many **Church Fathers and medieval** writers, Augustine among them, read the story as a detailed allegory of salvation: the traveller is Adam, the Samaritan is Christ, the inn is the church. Most Reformation and modern interpreters treat it chiefly as an answer to the lawyer's question.
- Many **modern** readings, Catholic and Protestant, stress its social teaching: practical mercy across ethnic and religious lines. Reformed readers affirm this duty while keeping the parable's law-and-gospel setting in view.

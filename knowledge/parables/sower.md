# Summary

"Behold, a sower went forth to sow". Some seed fell by the way side and the birds "devoured them up". Some fell on stony places and sprang up quickly, but "because they had no root, they withered away." Some fell among thorns, "and the thorns sprung up, and choked them". "But other fell into good ground, and brought forth fruit, some an hundredfold, some sixtyfold, some thirtyfold." Jesus ended, "Who hath ears to hear, let him hear."[^kjv-matt13-1]

# Setting

Jesus "sat by the sea side", and the crowds were so great that "he went into a ship, and sat; and the whole multitude stood on the shore."[^kjv-matt13-1] It opens a chapter of kingdom parables in Matthew; Mark 4 and Luke 8 also record it. The disciples asked, "Why speakest thou unto them in parables?" Jesus answered, "it is given unto you to know the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven, but to them it is not given", and applied Isaiah's words about those who "seeing see not".[^kjv-matt13-10] Then he explained the parable himself. Mark puts it briefly, "The sower soweth the word",[^kjv-mark4-14] and Luke, "The seed is the word of God."[^kjv-luke8-11]

# Meaning

The four grounds are four kinds of hearers. By the way side, the hearer "understandeth it not, then cometh the wicked one, and catcheth away that which was sown in his heart." On stony ground, he receives it "with joy", yet "dureth for a while" and falls away under persecution. Among thorns, "the care of this world, and the deceitfulness of riches, choke the word". The good ground is the one who "heareth the word, and understandeth it; which also beareth fruit".[^kjv-matt13-18] Luke adds that such hearers "in an honest and good heart, having heard the word, keep it, and bring forth fruit with patience."[^kjv-luke8-11]

Matthew Henry writes, "The seed sown is the word of God. The sower is our Lord Jesus Christ, by himself, or by his ministers." He finds the mark of the good ground in fruit: "What distinguished the good ground was fruitfulness. By this true Christians are distinguished from hypocrites." Yet "Christ does not say that this good ground has no stones in it, or no thorns; but none that could hinder its fruitfulness." His application is personal: "let us look to ourselves that we may know what sort of hearers we are."[^henry-matt13]

# Interpretive notes

In Reformed reading good ground is not natural to any heart; it is made by grace. Henry prays "that by his new-creating grace our hearts may become good ground".[^henry-mark4] Understanding the parable is itself a gift: "Happy are we, and for ever indebted to free grace, if the same thing that is a parable to others ... is a plain truth to us".[^henry-luke8] Calvin, discussing Matthew 13:11, says Christ "had reason to distinguish them from crowds who were blind", and that "he enables those whom he illumines with his Spirit to make such progress as to know his Word and understand it."[^calvin-john16]

Reformed readers take the stony-ground hearers, who "for a while believe, and in time of temptation fall away",[^kjv-luke8-11] as people whose faith was never rooted. Henry likens "Hypocrites" to "the stony ground".[^henry-matt13] On this reading the parable does not teach that the truly regenerate can be lost (see [perseverance](/doctrines/perseverance.md)).

These are summaries for orientation, not drawn from the corpus:

- **Arminian and Wesleyan** readers often take Luke 8:13 to describe real believers who later fall away.
- Many **modern scholars** stress the parable's promise about the [kingdom](/themes/kingdom-of-god.md): despite much lost seed, the harvest is certain and abundant.

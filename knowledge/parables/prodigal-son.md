# Summary

A man had two sons. The younger said, "Father, give me the portion of goods that falleth to me", went into a far country and "wasted his substance with riotous living". When famine came he was sent to feed swine, and "would fain have filled his belly with the husks that the swine did eat: and no man gave unto him."[^kjv-luke15-11] Then "he came to himself" and resolved to go home and say, "Father, I have sinned against heaven, and before thee". While he was "yet a great way off", his father "had compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him". The father called for "the best robe", a ring, shoes and the fatted calf, "For this my son was dead, and is alive again; he was lost, and is found."[^kjv-luke15-17]

The elder son, coming in from the field, "was angry, and would not go in". He complained that he had served many years and was never given a feast. The father answered, "Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine", and insisted, "It was meet that we should make merry, and be glad".[^kjv-luke15-25]

# Setting

Luke sets the parable in answer to a complaint. "Then drew near unto him all the publicans and sinners for to hear him." The religious leaders objected: "This man receiveth sinners, and eateth with them."[^kjv-luke15-1] Jesus replied with three parables of lost things: a sheep, a piece of silver and a son. The first two end on the same note: "joy shall be in heaven over one sinner that repenteth".[^kjv-luke15-3] Henry reads those two as showing "the Saviour's joy on their return to him".[^henry-luke15-1]

# Meaning

Matthew Henry says the parable "shows the nature of repentance, and the Lord's readiness to welcome and bless all who return to him", and that it "fully sets forth the riches of gospel grace". He reads the younger son's course as a picture of sin: "A sinful state is of departure and distance from God", "a wanting state", "a vile, slavish state", and at last "a state of death".[^henry-luke15-11]

The son's recovery "begins by his coming to himself. That is a turning point in the sinner's conversion." The father's running welcome shows that "The Lord meets him with unexpected tokens of his forgiving love."[^henry-luke15-17]

The elder brother is the point aimed at the murmurers. Henry writes, "In the latter part of this parable we have the character of the Pharisees, though not of them alone." He traces their resentment to "pride, self-preference, and ignorance of a man's own heart", yet notes that God's grace shines "in his tender and gentle bearing with peevish saints".[^henry-luke15-25]

# Interpretive notes

In Reformed reading the son's return is itself the fruit of grace, not a human first step. Henry says that at the turning point "The Lord opens his eyes, and convinces him of sin". He also reads the father's gifts in the light of the wider gospel: the returning sinner "is clothed in the robe of the Redeemer's righteousness, made partaker of the Spirit of adoption".[^henry-luke15-17] See [justification](/doctrines/justification.md) and [forgiveness](/themes/forgiveness.md).

These are summaries for orientation, not drawn from the corpus:

- **Roman Catholic** teaching often uses the parable to illustrate conversion and the sacrament of penance (reconciliation).
- **Eastern Orthodox** churches read it on the Sunday of the Prodigal Son before Lent, as a call to repentance and return to the Father.
- Some **liberal Protestant** readers argue that, since no mediator appears in the story, God forgives without any atonement. Reformed readers answer that one parable is not meant to state the whole doctrine of salvation.

# Summary

God placed the first man in the garden of Eden with one prohibition: of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil "thou shalt not eat of it: for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die."[^kjv-gen2-17] Tempted by the serpent, the woman "took of the fruit thereof, and did eat, and gave also unto her husband with her; and he did eat."[^kjv-gen3-6] The two hide from God, are judged and are sent out of the garden. Yet within the sentence on the serpent God promises that the woman's seed "shall bruise thy head, and thou shalt bruise his heel."[^kjv-gen3-15]

# Context

The Fall follows the account of creation, where man is made in God's image. Matthew Henry describes the first state of man: "Thus holy, thus happy, were our first parents in having the image of God upon them."[^henry-gen1-26] The command about the tree was a test: Henry writes that "everlasting life made sure to him upon his obedience".[^henry-gen2-16] Reformed theology calls this first arrangement the covenant of works, and Henry uses that language when Adam is sent out: it was now "in vain for him and his to expect righteousness, life, and happiness, by the covenant of works".[^henry-gen3-22]

Henry reads the serpent as the devil's instrument and traces the temptation's method: "Satan teaches men first to doubt, and then to deny."[^henry-gen3-1] Adam's act he sums up plainly: "His sin was, in one word, disobedience", and by it "He drew all his posterity into sin and ruin."[^henry-gen3-6]

# Significance

In Reformed reading the Fall explains the human condition. Adam acted as the head of the race, so that his guilt and corruption pass to all his descendants (original sin; see [sin](/themes/sin.md) and [Adam](/people/adam.md)). Paul writes that "by one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin; and so death passed upon all men, for that all have sinned".[^kjv-rom5-12] Henry comments: "Adam sinning, his nature became guilty and corrupted, and so came to his children. Thus in him all have sinned."[^henry-rom5-12]

The same chapter holds the first word of the gospel. Henry calls Genesis 3:15 "A gracious promise ... of Christ, as the Deliverer of fallen man from the power of Satan", adding that "no sooner was the wound given, than the remedy was provided and revealed."[^henry-gen3-14] Paul sets Adam and Christ side by side: "as by one man's disobedience many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous."[^kjv-rom5-19] Henry shows Christ's sufferings answering the curse point by point: "Thus is the plaster as wide as the wound."[^henry-gen3-16] See [the crucifixion](/events/crucifixion.md) and [justification](/doctrines/justification.md).

**Where other traditions differ.** These are summaries for orientation, not drawn from the corpus:

- **Eastern Orthodox** theology usually speaks of ancestral sin: Adam's descendants inherit mortality and a disordered nature, but not Adam's guilt.
- **Roman Catholic** teaching affirms original sin, but describes fallen human nature as wounded rather than wholly in bondage, and holds that baptism removes the guilt of original sin.
- **Arminian and Wesleyan** traditions affirm original sin and depravity, but teach a prevenient grace given to all that restores the ability to respond to the gospel.
- Many modern readers take Genesis 2-3 as figurative or non-historical. Reformed confessions treat Adam as a historical person and the Fall as a real event, though some in Reformed churches debate how literally the details are to be read.

# Summary

Judah went into exile in stages. In "the third year of the reign of Jehoiakim" Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon besieged Jerusalem and carried off young men "of the king's seed, and of the princes", among them Daniel.[^kjv-dan1-1] A later deportation took King Jehoiachin, and the priest Ezekiel saw his visions "among the captives by the river of Chebar".[^kjv-ezek1-1] Finally the Babylonian captain Nebuzar-adan "burnt the house of the LORD" and the houses of Jerusalem, the army "brake down the walls of Jerusalem", and the remnant was deported: "So Judah was carried away out of their land."[^kjv-2kgs25-8] The exiles' grief is remembered in the psalm: "By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept, when we remembered Zion."[^kjv-ps137-1]

The exile ended under the Persians. "In the first year of Cyrus king of Persia" the LORD "stirred up the spirit of Cyrus", who proclaimed that the Jews might "go up to Jerusalem" and "build the house of the LORD God of Israel".[^kjv-ezra1-1]

# Context

The exile came after long warning. The Chronicler says God sent his messengers "because he had compassion on his people", but "they mocked the messengers of God, and despised his words, and misused his prophets, until the wrath of the LORD arose against his people, till there was no remedy."[^kjv-2chr36-15]

Jeremiah set its length: "these nations shall serve the king of Babylon seventy years."[^kjv-jer25-11] To the exiles he wrote: "after seventy years be accomplished at Babylon I will visit you, and perform my good word toward you, in causing you to return to this place."[^kjv-jer29-10] Near the end Daniel "understood by books the number of the years" and turned to prayer.[^kjv-dan9-2] Isaiah had named Cyrus as the one who would say "to Jerusalem, Thou shalt be built".[^kjv-isa44-28] Matthew Henry reckons the seventy years "not ... from the last captivity, but the first".[^henry-jer29-8]

# Significance

The exile was judgment. Henry writes that "It was righteous with God to deprive those of the benefit of his worship, who had preferred false worships before it".[^henry-2kgs25]

It was also promise. Jeremiah's letter showed that God "had not forsaken them, though he was displeased, and corrected them."[^henry-jer29-1] At the return Henry sees that "though God's church be cast down, it is not cast off; though his people be corrected, they are not abandoned".[^henry-2chr36-22] Ezekiel's valley of dry bones was given, Henry says, "to encourage the desponding Jews" and "to predict ... their restoration after the captivity".[^henry-ezek37]

In Reformed reading the return points beyond itself to Christ. Calvin writes that "the return of the people from exile is connected with the restoration of the church accomplished by Christ", so that "the redemption is one."[^calvin-isa43] The restoration was "the prelude of the true and real redemption truly manifested in the person of Christ".[^calvin-joel3] Like [the exodus](/events/exodus.md), it is part of the story of God's [covenant](/themes/covenant.md) faithfulness; see also [sin](/themes/sin.md) and the book of [Isaiah](/books/isaiah.md).

**Where other traditions differ.** These are summaries for orientation, not drawn from the corpus:

- **Jewish** tradition mourns the destruction of both temples on the fast of the Ninth of Av; many historians see the exile as the time that reshaped Judaism around the Torah.
- **Critical scholars** commonly date Isaiah 40-66 to the exile and Daniel to the second century BC, and so read the naming of Cyrus and Daniel's visions as written after the events. The Reformed tradition receives them as genuine prophecy. Historians usually date the fall of Jerusalem to 587 or 586 BC and the decree of Cyrus to about 538 BC.

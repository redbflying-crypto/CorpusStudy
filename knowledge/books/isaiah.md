# Overview

Isaiah is the first of the major prophets. It moves from judgment on a rebellious people to comfort, and ends with God's promise: "behold, I create new heavens and a new earth".[^kjv-isa65-17] Matthew Henry notes that Isaiah "has been well called the evangelical prophet, on account of his numerous and full prophesies concerning the coming and character, the ministry and preaching, the sufferings and death of the Messiah".[^henry-isa-intro]

# Setting

The book presents itself as "The vision of Isaiah the son of Amoz, which he saw concerning Judah and Jerusalem in the days of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah."[^kjv-isa1-1] That places Isaiah in Jerusalem in the eighth century BC, when Assyria threatened Judah. From chapter 40 the horizon moves to the exile in Babylon and the return. Henry reads these chapters as looking further still: "Under the veil of the deliverance from Babylon, Isaiah points to a much greater deliverance, which was to be effected by the Messiah".[^henry-isa-intro] The Reformed tradition has generally held to the unity of the book under Isaiah's name. John, for example, quotes Isaiah 53:1 and Isaiah 6:10 side by side and ascribes both to "Esaias the prophet".[^kjv-john12-38]

**Where other traditions differ.** These are summaries for orientation, not drawn from the corpus. Most critical scholars divide the book between Isaiah of Jerusalem (much of chapters 1-39), a prophet of the exile (40-55) and later writers (56-66), chiefly because the later chapters address the exile and name Cyrus. Some who accept this division still read the whole book as Scripture with one message. Jewish interpreters commonly read the Servant of Isaiah 53 as Israel rather than the Messiah.

# Outline

- **Chapters 1-12:** judgment and hope for Judah; Isaiah's call; the promised child.
- **Chapters 13-27:** oracles against the nations and the judgment of the whole earth.
- **Chapters 28-35:** woes on Judah's leaders and the coming King.
- **Chapters 36-39:** Hezekiah, Assyria's defeat and the warning of Babylon.
- **Chapters 40-55:** comfort for exiles, the folly of idols, and the Servant of the LORD.
- **Chapters 56-66:** the true people of God and the new creation.

# Themes

- **The holiness of God.** The seraphim cry, "Holy, holy, holy, is the LORD of hosts", and the prophet confesses, "Woe is me! for I am undone".[^kjv-isa6-3] John says Isaiah "saw his glory, and spake of him".[^kjv-john12-38] Calvin declines to restrict the vision to the person of Christ, yet agrees "it is correct to say that Isaiah saw the glory of Christ, because Christ was the image of the invisible God."[^calvin-isa6] See [holiness](/themes/holiness.md).
- **Immanuel.** To faithless Ahaz the Lord gives a sign: "Behold, a virgin shall conceive, and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel."[^kjv-isa7-14] Matthew applies it to the birth of Jesus: "they shall call his name Emmanuel, which being interpreted is, God with us."[^kjv-matt1-23] Henry reads it the same way: "of you the Messiah is to be born, and you cannot be destroyed while that blessing is in you."[^henry-isa7]
- **The coming King.** "For unto us a child is born, unto us a son is given".[^kjv-isa9-6] Henry asks, "To what earthly king or kingdom can these words apply?"[^henry-isa9] See [the kingdom of God](/themes/kingdom-of-god.md).
- **Comfort and pardon.** "Comfort ye, comfort ye my people ... her iniquity is pardoned".[^kjv-isa40-1] Henry sees here "the great atonement of the death of Christ".[^henry-isa40]
- **The suffering Servant.** "He was wounded for our transgressions ... and the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all."[^kjv-isa53-5] Philip, reading Isaiah 53, "preached unto him Jesus."[^kjv-acts8-35] Calvin draws from it "the general doctrine that we are freely reconciled to God because Christ paid the price of our peace."[^calvin-isa53-5] See [atonement](/doctrines/atonement.md) and [the crucifixion](/events/crucifixion.md).

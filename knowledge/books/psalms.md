# Overview

The Psalms are 150 songs and prayers, Israel's hymnbook and the church's (see [worship](/themes/worship.md)). They run from "Blessed is the man that walketh not in the counsel of the ungodly"[^kjv-ps1-1] to "Let every thing that hath breath praise the LORD."[^kjv-ps150-6] Matthew Henry calls them "the language of the believer's heart, whether mourning for sin, thirsting after God, or rejoicing in Him", and notes that "no part of the Old Testament is more frequently quoted or referred to in the New."[^henry-ps-intro]

# Setting

Henry writes that "David was the penman of most of the psalms, but some evidently were composed by other writers, and the writers of some are doubtful", adding that "all were written by the inspiration of the Holy Ghost".[^henry-ps-intro] The collection grew over centuries. One early collection closes with the note "The prayers of David the son of Jesse are ended."[^kjv-ps72-20] The book is arranged in five parts (Psalms 1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150), several of which end with a doxology such as "Blessed be the LORD God of Israel from everlasting, and to everlasting. Amen, and Amen."[^kjv-ps41-13]

**Where other traditions differ.** These are summaries for orientation, not drawn from the corpus. Many critical scholars doubt that the psalm titles reliably name authors, and date much of the collection after the exile. Readers also differ on how far the Psalms speak of Christ: Jewish readers hear them in Israel's own history and hope, and some Christian interpreters limit messianic readings to the psalms the New Testament quotes that way. Within the Reformed family, some churches sing only psalms in worship, while most also sing hymns.

# Outline

- **Book I (Psalms 1-41)** and **Book II (42-72):** mostly psalms of David, with psalms of the sons of Korah; Psalms 1 and 2 are often read as an introduction to the whole.
- **Book III (73-89):** psalms of Asaph and others, ending with the lament over David's line in Psalm 89.
- **Book IV (90-106):** God's reign and faithfulness.
- **Book V (107-150):** thanksgiving, the songs of ascent, Psalm 119 on the word, and closing praise.

# Themes

- **The word of God.** Calvin draws from Psalm 1:2 that "The standard for religion must be taken from God's Word."[^calvin-ps1] See [Scripture](/doctrines/scripture.md).
- **God's care.** "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want."[^kjv-ps23-1] Calvin reads the shepherd as a picture of providence: "God's care for those who are his own is like the solicitude of a shepherd for the sheep intrusted to him."[^calvin-ps23]
- **Sin and repentance.** David prays, "blot out my transgressions" and "Against thee, thee only, have I sinned".[^kjv-ps51-1] Henry notes that "the truly penitent will ever trace back the streams of actual sin to the fountain of original depravity."[^henry-ps51]
- **Christ in the Psalms.** Jesus speaks of what was written "in the psalms, concerning me."[^kjv-luke24-44] On the cross he took up Psalm 22: "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?"[^kjv-ps22-1][^kjv-matt27-46] Henry reads that psalm as testifying "the sufferings of Christ, and the glory that should follow."[^henry-ps22] Of Psalm 110, "Sit thou at my right hand",[^kjv-ps110-1] Henry writes: "Christ shall not only be a King, but a Priest."[^henry-ps110] In Reformed reading, as Henry puts it, "Every psalm either points directly to Christ ... or may lead the believer's thoughts to Him."[^henry-ps-intro] See [David](/people/david.md) and [the kingdom of God](/themes/kingdom-of-god.md).

# Key passages

Psalm 1 (the two ways); Psalm 2 (the LORD's anointed); Psalm 22 (the forsaken sufferer); Psalm 23 (the LORD my shepherd); Psalm 51 (a penitent's prayer); Psalm 110 (priest and king); Psalm 150 (closing praise).

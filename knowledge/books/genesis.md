# Overview

Genesis is the first book of the Bible and of the five books of the Law. Matthew Henry notes that its Greek name signifies "the book of generation or production", fitting a book "containing an account of the origin of all things".[^henry-gen-intro] It opens with "In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth"[^kjv-gen1-1] and closes with Joseph's death in Egypt and his confidence that "God will surely visit you, and bring you out of this land".[^kjv-gen50-24] Between the two, the story narrows from the whole human race to one family through whom God promises to bless "all families of the earth".[^kjv-gen12-1]

# Setting

Jewish and Christian tradition, the Reformed churches included, has received Genesis as the first book of Moses. Calvin comments on it in those terms: "Moses meant simply to say that the world at its beginning was not the finished product we see today".[^calvin-gen1] The book itself names no author or date. Its events run from creation to the settling of Jacob's family in Egypt.

**Where other traditions differ.** These are summaries for orientation, not drawn from the corpus. Many modern critical scholars regard Genesis as compiled from several sources and completed long after Moses. Readers also differ over Genesis 1-11: some take the creation days as ordinary days, others (including some within Reformed churches) as long ages or as a literary framework, and others read the early chapters as non-historical. Reformed confessions affirm creation by God from nothing and a historical Adam and fall.

# Outline

The book is marked by repeated headings such as "These are the generations of the heavens and of the earth".[^kjv-gen2-4] Its main parts are:

- **Chapters 1-11:** creation, the [fall](/events/fall.md), Cain and Abel, the flood and Babel.
- **Chapters 12-25:** the call of [Abraham](/people/abraham.md), God's covenant with him and the birth of Isaac.
- **Chapters 25-36:** Isaac, Jacob and Esau, and Jacob's renaming as Israel.
- **Chapters 37-50:** Joseph, sold into Egypt and raised up to save his family.

Henry marks the turn at chapter 12: "From henceforward Abram and his seed are almost the only subject of the history in the Bible."[^henry-gen12]

# Themes

- **Creation.** Calvin reads the verb "create" to mean that "the world was created from nothing".[^calvin-gen1] See [creation](/themes/creation.md).
- **Fall and promise.** After the first sin God says the woman's seed "shall bruise thy head".[^kjv-gen3-15] In Reformed reading, with much of the Christian tradition, this is the first promise of Christ; Henry calls it "A gracious promise ... of Christ, as the Deliverer of fallen man from the power of Satan."[^henry-gen3-14]
- **Covenant and election.** Henry writes that "God made choice of Abram, and singled him out from among his fellow-idolaters".[^henry-gen12] Abram "believed in the LORD; and he counted it to him for righteousness."[^kjv-gen15-6] Henry adds that justifying faith "always has respect to the person and work of Christ".[^henry-gen15] See [covenant](/themes/covenant.md) and [justification](/doctrines/justification.md).
- **Providence.** Joseph tells his brothers: "ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good".[^kjv-gen50-20] Calvin draws from the story that "God directs men's confused and turbulent movements to a good end", while "his providence is free from all iniquity".[^calvin-gen45]

# Key passages

Genesis 1 (creation); Genesis 3 (the fall and the first promise); Genesis 12:1-3 (the call of Abram); Genesis 15:1-6 (faith counted for righteousness); Genesis 22:1-19 (the binding of Isaac); Genesis 50:15-21 (God meant it for good).
